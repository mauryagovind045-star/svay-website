Philosophy

Why every room begins with Self

Svay comes from the Sanskrit स्वयम् — Svayam, the self. It is an easy thing to put on a studio wall and a harder thing to design by. Here is what it actually changes.

Simran Siriskar · November 2026 · 4 min read

Most design conversations start with a reference image. Someone opens a saved folder, and within a minute there is a wall of rooms belonging to other people — rooms that photograph well, in houses with different light, different weather and different lives in them.

We start somewhere slower. Before any moodboard, we want to know how you actually live: which room you walk into first when you get home, where you eat when nobody is watching, what you own that you would never give away, how late you work, whether the house is quiet or full.

A home should look like the person who lives in it — not like the last house we designed.

Taste is not the same as self

Taste is what you like when you are browsing. Self is what you need when you are tired. The two overlap, but they are not the same, and a house built only on the first one tends to age badly — it photographs beautifully in month one and feels like someone else's by month eight.

So we test preferences against habits. A client who loves pale upholstery but has two dogs is not wrong about the colour; they are wrong about the fabric. A couple who want a formal dining table but eat on the sofa every night need a better sofa and a smaller table. The brief often contains its own correction, if you listen long enough to hear it.

What this looks like in practice

Three things follow from designing this way.

We ask about routine before aesthetics

Where the light falls at the hour you are actually home matters more than where it falls at noon. We plan seating, storage and lighting around the day as it is lived, not as it is imagined.

We keep what is already yours

Nearly every home we work on has a few pieces with history — a grandmother's trunk, a chair that has moved cities four times, art bought on a trip. These are not obstacles to a scheme. They are usually the most personal thing in the room, and we would rather build the palette around them than quietly retire them.

We design for the quiet hours

A room has to work on a Tuesday evening with nobody visiting. That is the test we keep coming back to: not whether it impresses, but whether it is easy to be in.

Why it matters more here

In Goa especially, the temptation is to design the postcard — all breeze and rattan and white linen. It is a real part of living here, but it is not the whole of it. Houses here deal with humidity, hard afternoon light, salt in the air and seasons that swing between still heat and heavy rain. A space designed around the person who lives in it tends to also be a space designed around those realities, because the two questions are asked at the same time.

The result rarely looks like a trend. It looks like a serene, intentional home — one where every decision traces back to something true about the people in it. That is the whole idea behind the name.
